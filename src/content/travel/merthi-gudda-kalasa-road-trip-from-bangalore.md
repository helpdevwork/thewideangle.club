Some trips need planning, and some just need a long weekend and enough bikes. The Gandhi Jayanti
break on 2nd October gave us three days with no real obligation, and nine of us decided to point
ourselves toward Kalasa — a small temple town in Chikkamagaluru district where the Western Ghats
get properly dense and the air smells like wet earth and wild cardamom. The draw was Merthi Gudda,
a peak sitting at roughly 2,000 metres above sea level that's been gaining traction among
Bangalore's weekend trekking crowd without yet attracting the queues and permit hassles of the
more famous Karnataka summits. Six bikes, three pillions, one WhatsApp group with too many voice
notes, and a loose plan that mostly amounted to "ride there, trek up, figure out the rest" — that
was the entire itinerary going in, and honestly it held up better than most spreadsheet-planned
trips I've been on.

## Day 1: Bangalore to Kalasa via Hassan

We rolled out of Bangalore somewhere around 6:30 a.m. on the morning of 2nd October. Six bikes in
a loose convoy through the city's early-morning traffic, which at that hour on a national holiday
was about as forgiving as Bangalore traffic ever gets. The first proper stop was Hassan, roughly
three hours down NH 75, where we'd arranged breakfast at a colleague's house. This wasn't a quick
chai-and-move kind of stop — his family had laid out a full South Indian breakfast, and the nine of
us descended on it with the appetite of people who'd been riding since dawn without eating. Hassan
itself is one of those Karnataka towns that most Bangalore riders blow past on the way to Chikmagalur
or Sakleshpur, but it's a perfectly reasonable place to break the ride and reset before the roads
start climbing into the Ghats.

From Hassan, we picked up the route toward Belur and then cut southwest toward Kalasa. The roads
through this stretch are the kind that remind you why a bike trip beats a car for this part of
Karnataka — narrow enough that the canopy closes over in places, with the occasional sharp turn
that opens up into a valley view you weren't expecting. The ride quality stays decent through most
of it, though the last thirty-odd kilometres into Kalasa throw in a few patches where the monsoon
has done its usual editorial work on the tarmac.

We pulled into Kalasa at around 4 p.m. — roughly nine and a half hours door to door from Bangalore,
including the long breakfast halt. For a ride of that distance, it didn't feel punishing. The last
hour of approach, winding through areca and coffee plantations with the Ghats filling the
windshield, does a lot to take the edge off any fatigue that's built up.

## The stay: Merthi Gudda Homestay

We'd booked Merthi Gudda Homestay, a small property close enough to the trailhead that the next
morning's trek wouldn't require an early-morning drive on top of the hike. The cost came to ₹1,700
per head per night, which for two nights worked out to ₹3,400 (give or take the usual rounding that
happens when nine people try to split bills on GPay). For a homestay in this part of Karnataka
during a long weekend, that's a reasonable rate — not the cheapest floor-and-mattress setup, but
nowhere near the boutique-stay pricing that some of the trendier Chikmagalur properties have
started charging.

The property itself was simple and functional: clean rooms, enough hot water for nine people to
cycle through showers without a mutiny, and a host who clearly knew the area well enough to brief
us on the trek route without needing to pull up Google Maps. There's no resort-style landscaping
or infinity pool here — the setting does the work, and the setting is a hillside in the Western
Ghats surrounded by the kind of green that only exists in places that get properly rained on for
four months straight.

## Evening of Day 1: the jhari and the leeches

After dumping bags and sorting out rooms, the nine of us went exploring the area around the
homestay before the light dropped. A short walk downhill turned up a small jhari — a narrow stream
of water flowing through rocks and moss, not quite a waterfall but more than a trickle, the kind
of incidental water feature that wouldn't make any travel guide but was exactly what tired legs
needed after a full day on a bike. We found a good spot, pulled off shoes, and soaked our feet in
the cold water while the forest did its thing around us.

The leeches showed up about ten minutes in. This is Malnad country in the weeks just after monsoon,
so leeches aren't a surprise — they're a guarantee. Small, persistent, and weirdly stealthy, they
made their presence felt on ankles and between toes, prompting the usual round of controlled panic,
salt applications, and "wait, is that one on you too?" conversations that are apparently mandatory
on any Western Ghats trip between September and November. Nobody got dramatically bled, but a few
of us carried small red souvenirs back to the homestay as proof of encounter. If leeches genuinely
bother you, long socks tucked into trousers and a salt shaker in your daypack are the standard
Malnad precautions — they work, mostly.

We came back to the stay, cleaned up, and spent the rest of the evening doing what groups of nine
do at a homestay with no TV and patchy cell signal: talking, eating whatever the host put in front
of us, and going to bed early enough to not regret the next morning's alarm.

## Day 2: The Merthi Gudda trek

The trek was the whole point of the trip, and it delivered. We started the climb at around 8:30
a.m. after a quick breakfast, and the trail from near the homestay heads more or less straight up
through dense forest before thinning out into grassland and exposed rock as you gain altitude. The
path is reasonably well-marked — there's enough foot traffic now that the route is obvious for most
of the climb — but it's steep enough in places that anyone who hasn't walked uphill in a while will
feel it in their calves within the first half hour.

The forest section in the lower half is where the trek earns its atmosphere. Tall trees with moss
running up the trunks, the sound of water somewhere off the trail but never quite visible, and a
canopy thick enough that the morning light comes through in patches rather than sheets. Birdcalls
are constant but identifying anything beyond a Malabar whistling thrush or a bulbul was beyond our
collective expertise. The air is noticeably cooler under the canopy than it was at the homestay,
and it carries that particular wet-forest smell — decomposing leaves, damp bark, something vaguely
floral — that's hard to describe and impossible to fake.

The upper section opens up into shola grassland, and that's where the views start. On a clear day
— which we got, or close to it — the panorama from the ridgeline takes in a sweep of the
Chikkamagaluru Ghats that makes the climb feel entirely worth it. We reached the summit area at
around 10:30, roughly two hours from the start, which is about average for a group of mixed
fitness levels that stops for photos every time the view changes.

We spent close to an hour and a half at the top, eating the lunch we'd carried up (packed by the
homestay host, simple but filling), sitting on rocks, and taking the kind of photos that look
incredible on a phone screen and somehow never quite capture the scale of what you're actually
looking at. The wind at the top was strong enough to be refreshing without being unpleasant, and
the cloud patterns moving across the valleys below gave the landscape a shifting quality that made
it hard to stop watching.

We started the descent at around 12:30. Going down is faster but harder on the knees, and the
steeper sections of trail that felt fine on the way up turned into careful foot-placement exercises
on the way down. The whole group was back at the base by early afternoon, tired in the good way
that only a morning spent walking uphill can produce.

## Rudrapada and the Bhadra River

After the trek, we drove out to Rudrapada — a spot along the Bhadra River a short distance from
Kalasa. The Bhadra is one of Karnataka's significant rivers, originating in the Western Ghats near
Gangamoola (not far from where the Tunga also begins), and by the time it reaches Rudrapada it's
wide enough and deep enough in places to properly swim in rather than just wade through.

We'd originally planned to visit the hanging bridge nearby as well, but it was closed — whether
for maintenance or for the season, we never got a clear answer. The river itself more than made up
for it. Eight of the nine of us got in (the ninth exercised a right to sit on rocks and observe,
which in fairness is a perfectly valid way to experience a river), and the water was exactly the
right temperature after a morning of trekking — cold enough to feel restorative, not cold enough
to feel punishing. The riverbed at Rudrapada has a mix of smooth rock and sand, and the current at
the spot we picked was strong enough to feel interesting without being dangerous. An hour in the
water, drying off on warm rocks afterward, and watching the river move through the valley — it was
the kind of unstructured afternoon that a trip like this is really for, and it needed zero planning
to work.

## The non-veg situation (or lack thereof)

This is the part of the trip report that will matter to anyone who, like our group, treats
non-vegetarian food as a core travel requirement rather than an optional upgrade. Kalasa is
predominantly vegetarian territory. The town's identity is anchored by the Kalaseshwara Temple, and
the food scene reflects that — finding a non-veg restaurant in and around Kalasa during our stay
proved genuinely difficult, to the point where we eventually gave up the search and leaned into
what was available.

What was available turned out to be surprisingly good, if unexpected. We found Yatra Cafe, a
vegetarian restaurant that served, among other things, a paneer dosa — a combination that none of
us had encountered before and that sounded wrong enough to be interesting. It was, in fact, very
good. The cafe was also the place where we refuelled energy-wise after the trek, eating more than
any of us would normally order at a single sitting because the combination of a two-hour climb,
river swimming, and a morning that had started before 7 a.m. had left the group operating at a
caloric deficit that only volume could fix.

The non-veg drought finally broke on the way home. But more on that in the Day 3 section.

## Evening of Day 2: Uno and early lights-out

Back at the homestay, the evening followed the same low-key pattern as the first night. Showers,
a change into dry clothes, and a group Uno session that lasted longer and got more competitive
than anyone would have predicted. Uno with nine people is chaotic in a way that the game's
designers probably didn't intend, but it works perfectly as the kind of activity that fills the
gap between dinner and sleep when everyone's too tired to do anything ambitious but not quite
ready to turn in. Dinner was served at the homestay, we ate more than we needed to, and the
group was asleep by a time that would be embarrassingly early to admit in any other context.

## Day 3: The ride home and the Imperio payoff

We checked out of the homestay the next morning and started the ride back to Bangalore at around
8 a.m. The return route retraced the outbound one — back through the plantation roads, through
Belur, and into Hassan, where the trip's biggest food moment was waiting.

Hassan's Imperio restaurant was the stop, and it was worth every kilometre of the detour off the
highway. After two and a half days of enforced vegetarianism in Kalasa (enjoyable, but felt), the
group walked into Imperio with the focused determination of people who had been thinking about
chicken since the previous afternoon. The food delivered completely — proper Karnataka-style
non-veg that hit the exact spot the trip had been missing. Without overstating it, the meal at
Imperio might have been the single most satisfying eating experience of the entire three days,
partly because the food was genuinely excellent and partly because of the sheer relief of finally
getting what we'd been craving.

We took long breaks on the ride back — the urgency that pushes you forward on the outbound leg
doesn't exist on the return, and with a belly full of Imperio's cooking, nobody was in a rush.
The group rolled back into Bangalore somewhere around 6 p.m., which makes the return a roughly
ten-hour affair including stops. Not fast, but nobody was racing.

## Practical notes

**Getting there:** Bangalore to Kalasa is approximately 350 km via Hassan and Belur. The NH 75
stretch to Hassan is a comfortable highway ride; beyond that, the roads narrow and wind but stay
in reasonable condition. Budget nine to ten hours with stops.

**Best time to visit:** October through March. The monsoon (June–September) makes the trek
slippery and the leeches significantly worse, and some homestays close or reduce capacity during
peak rain. October, right after the monsoon lifts, is arguably the best window — everything is at
maximum green, the watercourses are full, and the air is cool without being cold.

**Costs:** ₹3,400 per head for two nights at Merthi Gudda Homestay (₹1,700/night). Fuel costs
for a Bangalore round trip on a bike will vary by mileage, but ₹800–1,200 is a reasonable
estimate for most 150–200cc bikes. Food was inexpensive throughout — meals in Kalasa and Hassan
ran well under ₹300 per person per meal.

**Trek difficulty:** Moderate. The Merthi Gudda trek is manageable for anyone with basic fitness
— no technical climbing, no ropes, no permits required at the time of our visit. Allow two hours
up and about ninety minutes down. Carry water, sunscreen for the exposed upper section, and a
packed lunch if your homestay offers it.

**Leeches:** Real, present, and unavoidable in the weeks after monsoon. Long socks, tucked-in
trousers, and salt are your standard defenses. They're a nuisance, not a danger.

**Food:** Kalasa is vegetarian-dominant. If non-veg is important to you, eat well in Hassan on
the way in or out — Imperio is a reliable option. In Kalasa itself, Yatra Cafe serves solid
vegetarian food with a few creative options like paneer dosa.
